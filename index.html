What is queer life in Chicago?

By Jake Cox

Every month, about 400 women pack into the Annoyance Theatre in Lakeview for Lipstick Lounge, a party for queer women and femmes thrown by Dama Productions. Slideshows run all night. Between glamour shots, the screens flash short messages: “take care of each other.” “Buy her a drink.” “Enjoy yourself tonight.”

Kendra Jamaica, the 33-year-old sapphic Latine promoter behind the party, calls it subliminal messaging; she believes there is so much power in the impressions you can make by curating a party.

“People will remember how you make them feel,” she said, paraphrasing Maya Angelou. In a year of monthly parties, she said, there has never been a fight; strangers even cover each other in coat check.

The scene on State Street

A century ago, the center of queer nightlife in Chicago wasn’t on the North Side. It was in Bronzeville on the Southside.

From the 1920s through the 1940s, basement ballrooms and cabarets along State Street hosted drag balls and female impersonator revues that drew hundreds of Chicagoans across lines of race and sexuality.

The Cabin Inn, a Black-owned cabaret, made female impersonators its headline act. Finnie’s Ball, the annual Halloween drag ball, became one of the largest queer public events in the country, running for years.

And while in some ways this scene was strong — it went as quickly as it came. Postwar policing, urban renewal and decades of disinvestment unwound it piece by piece. Today, only traces remain; in buildings that now remain empty and one bastion of that time: The Jeffery Pub in South Shore, one of the country’s longest-running Black-owned gay bars.

The darkest tracts cluster along the North Side lakefront — miles from the State Street corridor where the city’s queer nightlife began.

Two Chicagos

The geographic center of LGBTQ+ Chicago, as most people now understand it, sits miles north. Northalsted, formerly Boystown, was officially recognized by Mayor Richard M. Daley in 1997, making it the first officially designated “gay district” in the United States.

But for many younger queer Chicagoans, that designation describes a neighborhood more than a community.

Safiya Baker, a DePaul alum and lesbian who lives on the North Side, draws a sharp line between two cultures that share an acronym.

“Gay Chicago, I think, is like the Boystown, cis, muscled, white gays,” she said. “That type of gays that have money or are very well-to-do.”

Baker said queer Chicago, to her, centers around the city’s youth.

“It’s definitely more ethnically diverse, more gender diverse,” she said. “They tend to be more creative with their jobs ... more folks that are focusing on expressions of art and expressions of themselves.”

The divide is cultural, but it’s also geographic — and increasingly, Baker said, the queer side of it doesn’t live at any address at all. To find the spaces she’s looking for, she doesn’t follow venues.

“Chicago is more, you have to follow the DJ more than the venue,” she said.

Jamaica, who has spent more than a decade as a self-described “club kid,” came to the same conclusion from the other side of the door.

“It’s no longer about the venues, it’s no longer about the neighborhoods, it’s about the collectives,” she said. “Now it’s for the indies.”

It’s a theme that would have been familiar to anyone who followed the drag balls from ballroom to ballroom on State Street a century ago — a scene organized around performers and word of mouth, not bar owners.

Jamaica knows the fixed-address version intimately. She used to live in Boystown. The spaces that formed her were the exceptions within it: Queen, the legendary drag-hosted Sunday party at Smart Bar, and Berlin, the Belmont Avenue club that closed in 2023.

“Berlin was the only place in Chicago where you didn’t have to be a cis man to be the main character that night,” she said. “The lesbians were taking over that place. I just remember listening to new wave music on a Saturday night in Boystown, which doesn’t necessarily happen too often.”

Though she’s careful to push back on sweeping characterizations of older queer people. For a queer person arriving from a small town that offered them nothing, she said, a Boystown bar might be the first room where they recognize themselves. Let that be an entry point, she said, and keep seeking.

“Who am I to tell someone on their journey, hey, you’re not queer enough?” she said. “That’s the most hurtful thing that you can do when someone finally musters the courage to come out.”

Joy is the whole point

Ask Jamaica why parties matter when queer, trans people and Latine people, are under escalating political attack, and she is very clear — at a certain point you just need joy.

“How are you going to pour from an empty cup? You’re just simply not,” she said. “By being able to relax, enjoy yourself, that’s how you’re able to fight the good fight. That’s how you have steam to make the things that you see missing.”

The joy, she’s quick to note, runs on unseen labor. For two years, Dama Productions, which she runs, was a one-woman operation: graphics, booking, promotion, emceeing, photography. Every show she’s launched started with her wanting to see something new, and she made it happen.

“These institutions need us more than we need them,” she said. “It’s a win-win for them. We’re doing all the labor, we’re making all the posters, we’re filling the seats, it’s a no-brainer.”

She also described that while collectives want to do more to reach audiences throughout the city, especially when asked about the south side, she said people need to help build what they want to see, as many promoters are already working day in and day out to keep events going.

“Then the collective is going to end,” she said, “and now we’re out of this beautiful space.”

The collectives, meanwhile, have organized themselves the way the old ballroom circuit once did — as a network. Chicago’s sapphic party organizers all communicate, Jamaica said, connecting Lipstick Lounge with collectives including Garden of Eden, Club Elixir and Starlight Sapphic.

Almost none of that history gets taught — even to the people looking for it.

Baker took an LGBTQ+ history course at DePaul specifically because she wanted to learn about her own community. The class covered Boystown, Andersonville and the Rainbow Coalition. The South Side didn’t come up, she added.

“It was definitely lacking a focus on more stories of joy, stories of people,” she said, “and less so focused on people struggling or suffering.”

That gap is not just an academic problem, said Heather Montes-Ireland, a professor of women’s and gender studies at DePaul. As legislators target drag performance and trans existence, the political project depends on the claim that queer and trans life is something new.

“People are trying to make a case that trans people are ahistoric. That’s hugely important to these nationalists and neo-fascists.”

Jamaica’s advice to anyone who looks around queer Chicago and finds something missing is the same advice she gave herself when she launched her first show: build it yourself.

“If you see something missing, make it,” she said.
